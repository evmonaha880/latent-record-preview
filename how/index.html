How it was made

Why I wrote it

I grew up in Rochester, New York, where George Eastman built Kodak. Outside Rochester he is mostly known for the company’s collapse, eighty years after his death, and I think that leaves out most of what he did.

I wrote this essay with Claude to give a fuller account. Claude drafted most of the sentences; I chose the argument and made every decision. This page keeps every draft of every paragraph, with the notes and messages that came before each change.

Evan chose the subject, the argument and every decision. Claude researched, drafted and checked. This is the method, in the order it happened.

Two Claude models did the writing: Claude Opus 5 from 3 to 19 August, then Claude Fable 5 from 19 to 21 August. A third, Claude Sonnet 5, ran one review on 4 August and changed nothing.

All times on this page are Pacific.

April 2025

It began with two nephews and a hard question

Evan was explaining how film works to his two nine-year-old nephews and could not find the words. So he used an AI, out loud, by voice, to explain the chemistry of film. That made him wonder how he could tell this whole story with AI.

The chemistry also gave the project its frame. A latent image is the picture that is already on the film but cannot be seen until it is developed.

December 2025

A question about Steve Jobs, then a biography ordered in one evening

The first question that December evening was about Steve Jobs and Xerox: was Rochester involved? Less than an hour later Evan asked Gemini to write a thorough biography of George Eastman and of Kodak. A long research report came back the same night.

March 2026

A trip with a supply chain question, and a vault that changed it

Evan is from Rochester and has been visiting the George Eastman house since he was a boy. In March 2026 he flew home with a narrow question: what does George Eastman look like from a supply chain point of view?

On 5 March he wrote to the University of Rochester's special collections and to two people at the Eastman Museum. A curator answered the next morning. Evan went to the university archive first. He had a camera and about an hour, and he photographed what he could: the original Kodak Primer, early advertisements, pages from a handwritten notebook, a case of cameras.

That afternoon the curator took him into the vault. He saw the original lunar photo lab output, and every camera and product Kodak had ever made. He sat with the curator and asked question after question.

8 June 2026

The first instruction

The first instruction of the project asked for a George Eastman biography modeled on the work of Erik Larson, Robert Caro and Patrick Radden Keefe. Evan typed it on 8 June. That evening Claude turned Caro's book about his own method, Working, into a style guide Evan could point to by name. He handed over the first sources the same night: Ackerman's 1930 biography, a record of Kodak's defense and aerospace work, and the book about the Polaroid war. Keefe's guide followed on 14 June.

3 August

Ten ideas, and none of them won

Ten candidate theses were on the table by mid afternoon on 3 August. Evan and Claude ran all ten through /story, a storytelling coach Evan had built on 30 July. It checks a draft beat by beat against a library of storytelling craft, suggests moves, and only rewrites when Evan says go. He ran it again on the argument on 4 August and on a full read-through on 15 August.

None of the ten won. At 7:36 pm Evan wrote the line that did: what is feared more is him being completely forgotten about to history.

Then he got stuck on what the Edison story was for. He told Claude to reread the letter he had written to a reader in June, the paragraph about Edison and the peep show. Claude's answer took a minute: Edison thought he was building a cartel. Eastman was building a tollbooth. It went into the thesis that night and into the essay the next afternoon. It has not moved since. It is paragraph 12.

At 10:17 that night the essay existed: a first save of 2,257 words, called The Argument.

The thesis page shows all ten ideas, and the one that landed.

3 to 21 August

He wrote in the margins; Claude rewrote

Most of the sentences are Claude's. Evan steered. He left notes in the margin saying what was wrong, struck out passages himself, sent chat messages with new ideas, and sometimes rewrote a line by hand. Claude produced the next draft. He read it and sent it round again. The grid beside every paragraph in the essay is the record of those rounds.

From 4 August the draft went through numbered passes. The record holds ten of them, passes 4 to 13. Six fell on 20 August alone. Pass 6 answered 38 of Evan's comments. Pass 9, on 20 August, answered 30 comments and 4 strikes.

20 to 21 August

The first reader, and one last fix

The essay went to its first reader on 20 August. Hours later, Claude looked at the photograph of Eastman's last note and found the essay said "seven words" when the note is three short lines. The fix was the last edit the essay ever got. The text has not changed since 21 August.

Reading the grids

It works like Wordle. Each row of squares is one draft of a paragraph, and each square is one sentence. Green means the sentence was kept as it was. Gold means it was reworded. Dark means it was cut. A red dot means a note of his changed the text. A blue dot means a chat message of his did.

The editor saved every few seconds and did not record who typed a given word, so the page never claims who wrote a particular word. It shows the note that came before each change, so you can see what was asked for and what came back.

What the record does not cover

  • Before 3 August. Fifteen months of research came first, much of it in other chat tools. Only pieces survive.
  • Two outside research runs. A reading of the MIT plaque (later found wrong) and a long research run on 19 August came from another tool and are not in the record.
  • Reading and thinking. What Claude read is logged with times. What Evan read on paper, and what he thought, are not.
  • Early readers. Their comments came back outside the record.
  • A few lost backups. Some backups from 4 August were deleted before the record was built.

The record was rebuilt from the raw save files, not from anyone's summary, and checked against them by program. Nothing touched the essay after its last save on 21 August.
